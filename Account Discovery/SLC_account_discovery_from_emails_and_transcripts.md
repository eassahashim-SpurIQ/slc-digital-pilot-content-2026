## Short Answer

SLC did not want a generic fintech list. They wanted accounts where SLC's product has a clear reason to matter: companies moving or protecting money/digital assets where users, customers, or employees authenticate or authorize high-value activity through a mobile device, wallet, app, or identity/security workflow.

The strongest direction became:

- wallet providers
- crypto firms and digital asset exchanges
- consumer wallets
- institutional wallet infrastructure
- wallet infrastructure providers
- institutional digital asset companies
- digital banks / crypto-friendly banks in priority regions
- money movement companies only where there is user/device authorization risk
- companies with live security, fraud, compliance, identity, or authentication signals

The team moved away from:

- pure B2B payment infrastructure
- backend payment rails
- companies where no end-user or employee device is involved
- targets where SLC cannot clearly attach itself to mobile/device authentication, fraud reduction, or transaction assurance

## Original Five Possible Segments

In the July 28 kickoff email, the possible segments were:

- wallets and payment providers

- banks and digital banks
- evidence-grade attestation
- telcos

These were kickoff options. The later meetings narrowed the practical first campaign toward wallets, payment/digital asset firms, and account types with strong signal evidence.

## What SLC Wanted Removed Or Avoided

Veena explicitly said to remove primarily B2B payment companies:

- Airwallex
- Payoneer
- Moneycorp
- Ebury
- 3S Money
- Tide
- MultiPass
- thinkmoney
- DiPocket

The reason was not that these companies are bad companies. The issue is fit:

- Pure B2B payments often do not have an end-user mobile device authorization flow.
- Backend payment rails may only move money from one side to another.
- Compliance responsibility may sit with their customer, not the infrastructure provider.
- SLC needs accounts where device-level trust/authentication is directly relevant.

Important nuance from Gavin:

Some B2B accounts could come back later if the data shows strong identity, trust, attestation, individual authorization, or risk signals. So B2B is not permanently impossible, but it should not be the first-pass target unless the signal proves fit.

## Account Discovery Signals SLC Cared About

### Phase 1 Signals

Use these first:

- newly licensed crypto firms in Europe
- new wallet firms appearing in government or regulatory registers
- companies whose help pages say they send codes by text / SMS
- wallets or apps with negative customer reviews about account takeover, missing money, unauthorized transactions, or phone-number changes
- companies hiring fraud, identity, security, compliance, or risk roles
- companies appearing in ESMA, FCA, CASP, or similar public registers

### Phase 2 Signals

Use these after the first live segment:

- newly launched consumer products
- regulator movement against text/SMS codes
- breach, fraud incident, SIM-swap, or public warning
- events, conference booths, speaking slots, and publication appearances
- G2 / CB Insights / analyst directories
- competitor customer lists such as Fireblocks, YubiKey, fraud-risk tools, hardware token providers
- social/media activity from senior and lower-tier influencers
- Reddit, Substack, GitHub, forums, and analyst-report engagement

## Personas To Target Inside Accounts

SLC did not want only one persona. They wanted several because each owns a different part of the problem.

Target personas:

- Chief Security Officer / CISO
- Chief Information Officer / CIO
- Chief Technology Officer / CTO
- Chief Compliance Officer
- Chief Risk Officer
- Risk and Compliance leaders
- Product leaders
- Identity / fraud / security hires
- Commercial or operational decision makers where money movement risk is relevant

Messaging should differ by persona:

- Compliance/risk: compliance, regulatory exposure, customer-money protection.
- Security/technology: securing customers and preventing account takeover or unauthorized approvals.
- Product/CX: reduce fraud without adding customer friction.
- Finance/operations: fraud cost, investigation cost, remediation, compensation, and operational load.

## Outreach Positioning From SLC

Lauren's email clarified that the outreach should sell value, not features.

The four value points she gave:

- reduce fraud and financial risk
- strengthen security beyond the login
- protect the customer experience
- improve security without replacing the existing stack

The message should not say only: "your name came up on a register."

Better approach:

- Start from a relevant business problem.
- Use the signal as context, not as the whole message.
- Make the copy simple and senior-person readable.
- Avoid overly technical language such as SCA, NCSC, possession factor, or step-up unless the audience clearly uses those terms.

## Practical Targeting Rule

For Airtable/Clay work, classify target accounts like this:

- Priority 1: wallet/digital asset/money movement company + SMS/text-code weakness or fraud review signal.
- Priority 2: wallet/digital asset/money movement company + recent identity/fraud/security/compliance hire.
- Priority 3: regulated wallet/crypto/digital asset account from ESMA/FCA/CASP/VARA/MAS/HK/AUSTRAC style registers.
- Priority 4: competitor customer or company attending/writing/speaking in relevant fraud, identity, wallet, fintech, or mobile-security spaces.
- Deprioritize: pure B2B payment rail unless there is explicit individual identity/attestation/device-authorization evidence.
